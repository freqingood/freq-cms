Episode purpose

Episode 6 looked at temporal density, how many events compete for perception over a given stretch of time. This episode widens that into the full picture: perceptual density in general, why adding tracks doesn't automatically make a mix feel bigger, and why the listener's limited attention is the real constraint every arrangement and mix decision is working against.

Script

Opening

Six episodes in, we've covered perception, loudness, fatigue, auditory objects, frequency masking and temporal masking. Here's a strange thing that sits on top of all of it.

You can add another guitar, another synth, another percussion layer, another vocal double, another pad, another harmony, another texture. And somehow the mix gets smaller. You added sound, but the result feels less spacious, less clear, less powerful, sometimes even less exciting.

So you turn things up. You add EQ. You widen something. You compress something. You add more contrast. And somehow the mix still feels crowded.

This seems contradictory. If more sounds are available, shouldn't the production feel bigger? Not necessarily. Because the listener doesn't experience your session as a track count. The listener experiences information. And when too much information arrives at once, the auditory system has to work harder to organise it.

That's today's subject: perceptual density. And this is one of the most important ideas for understanding why arrangement, production and mixing are really part of the same problem.

1. Track Count Isn't Perceptual Density

Let's begin with an important distinction. Imagine two productions.

Production A has four instruments:

  • Kick
  • Bass
  • Vocal
  • Guitar

But all four are active continuously. The guitar has dense chords, the bass is moving constantly, the vocal is almost continuous, the drums contain frequent fills.

Production B has twenty tracks. But many are silent most of the time, some appear only in the chorus, some sustain underneath, some answer the vocal, some reinforce particular moments, some disappear when another element becomes important.

Which one feels denser? Probably not the twenty-track production. Because track count is not the same thing as perceptual density.

2. What Is Perceptual Density?

We can use perceptual density as a practical framework for describing how much information is competing for the listener's attention at a given moment. That information can come from:

  • The number of simultaneous events
  • Spectral activity
  • Harmonic complexity
  • Rhythmic activity
  • Transient density
  • Changes in dynamics
  • Changes in register
  • Competing melodic lines
  • Overlapping phrases
  • Changes happening simultaneously

The important word is simultaneously. A production can contain enormous amounts of information over an entire song, but it doesn't have to present all of that information at the same moment. That distinction is crucial.

3. The Listener Has Limited Attention

Human perception is remarkably capable. We can listen to complex music, follow several instruments, understand speech in noisy environments, detect changes in rhythm, pitch, timbre and space. But this doesn't mean we consciously perceive every detail equally.

Attention is selective. The auditory system has to organise incoming information, it has to decide, in effect: what belongs together, what is foreground, what is background, what changed, what should I follow, what can I ignore? A dense arrangement gives the listener more decisions to make.

4. More Information Can Mean Less Information

Here's the paradox. Suppose you have one melody, it's clear. You add a second melody, still clear. Add a third, you can follow all three. Add a fourth, perhaps still manageable. Add several more, eventually the individual lines may become harder to follow.

You haven't removed any musical information, you've added information. But the listener's ability to identify each individual component may decrease. So more information can produce less perceptual distinction. That's the paradox of density.

5. This Is Why Arrangement Matters So Much

Arrangement is often treated as a compositional concern, mixing is then treated as the technical process that happens afterward. But from the listener's perspective, that distinction is artificial.

If five instruments play at the same time, the listener receives five streams of information. If one instrument drops out, the listener receives a different perceptual organisation. The arrangement has changed the problem before the mixer touches a fader. That's why the best mix decision can happen before the mix.

6. A Simple Listening Experiment

Take a chorus, listen normally. Now identify the most important musical element, maybe it's the lead vocal. Now mute one secondary element, perhaps a guitar, listen again. Mute another, then another.

At some point you may notice something surprising. The lead vocal doesn't necessarily sound louder, but it feels larger. Why? Because fewer things are competing with its information. You have created perceptual space without creating physical space.

7. Space Is Not Just Reverb

This distinction is extremely important. When people say "the mix needs more space," they often think:

  • More reverb
  • Wider panning
  • Longer delays
  • More stereo information

But there is another kind of space: informational space. A sound can have enormous room around it but still feel crowded if too many other events are competing with it. Conversely, a dry sound can feel enormous if very little else is asking for attention. So perceived space can come from what isn't happening.

8. Silence Creates Size

This connects directly to our earlier discussion of temporal masking. Silence isn't simply an absence of audio, it can create contrast. Imagine a drum fill that continues right up until a chorus. Now remove the final hit before the chorus, the chorus downbeat may suddenly feel bigger.

Nothing was added, you removed information immediately beforehand, and the important event gained perceptual contrast. This is one of the most powerful production techniques available: make the important moment easier to perceive by reducing what surrounds it.

9. Density Is Relative

A sound isn't "dense" by itself, density is contextual. One guitar playing a sustained chord may be extremely sparse. The same guitar playing rapid sixteenth-note voicings can be dense. A synthesizer holding one note can be sparse. A synth constantly changing:

  • Pitch
  • Filter
  • Envelope
  • Modulation
  • Rhythm

can be perceptually dense even if it is technically only one track. So when evaluating density, don't ask "how many sounds are there?" Ask "how much information is changing at this moment?"

10. Change Is Information

This is an important extension. A sustained sound may become part of the background, but a sudden change demands attention: a new note, a new rhythm, a transient, a pitch change, a register change, a new instrument, a change in dynamics, a change in articulation, a sudden silence. These events are informationally important. If too many things change simultaneously, the listener receives a large burst of new information.

11. The Chorus Problem

A common production instinct is "it's the chorus, add everything." So the chorus receives:

  • Additional guitars
  • Additional synths
  • Vocal doubles
  • Harmonies
  • Percussion
  • Cymbals
  • Extra bass layers
  • Strings
  • Effects
  • Fills

The chorus becomes objectively bigger in arrangement terms, but sometimes it feels smaller. Why? Because everything arrives simultaneously, there is no hierarchy, everything says "notice me." And when everything demands attention, nothing gets priority.

12. Hierarchy Is the Solution

A large production doesn't necessarily make every element equally important, instead it creates hierarchy. Something is foreground, something else is supporting, something else is background. Some things may become important briefly, then disappear. This creates movement. The listener doesn't have to process everything equally, they can follow the musical story.

13. The Listener Needs Stable References

This connects to another important perceptual principle: the listener benefits from stable references. Imagine a vocal, the listener uses it as a reference point, around it, instruments can change. If the vocal remains relatively stable while the arrangement moves, the listener can organise the changes around it.

But if vocal, bass, guitars, synths, drums and percussion are all changing constantly, there may be fewer stable anchors. The music can feel harder to follow.

14. Repetition Reduces Perceptual Load

This is why repetition is so powerful. The first time a musical phrase appears, the listener has to understand it. The second time, it is familiar. The third time, it becomes a reference. This means repetition doesn't necessarily make music less interesting, it can actually make complexity easier to perceive, because once one element becomes stable, the listener can devote attention to something new. This is one reason arrangement can introduce complexity gradually.

15. Complexity Needs Reference

Imagine an arrangement that changes completely every bar. The listener receives new harmony, new rhythm, new instrumentation, new register, new melody, new dynamics. It may be exciting, it may also become difficult to follow.

Now keep the bass pattern stable. Suddenly the harmonic and melodic changes become easier to hear. The bass became a reference. This is an important principle: complexity becomes easier to perceive when some information remains stable.

16. Why a Repeated Bass Line Can Make a Dense Arrangement Work

Suppose the bass repeats a simple figure. Above it:

  • Guitar changes
  • Vocal changes
  • Synth changes
  • Percussion changes

The bass acts as an anchor. It doesn't need to be the loudest element, its consistency makes the surrounding changes easier to organise. This is one reason arrangement isn't simply about adding variety, it is about balancing change and stability.

17. Density Can Be Harmonic

We've talked about rhythmic density, but harmony can also create density. Imagine a sustained chord. Four instruments play root, third, fifth, octave, this may feel relatively unified. Now give each instrument an independent melodic line, suddenly the same harmonic structure becomes much more active. The harmonic information hasn't necessarily become more complex, but the information being presented over time has increased.

18. Harmonic Density and Track Density Are Different

Ten tracks can reinforce one harmonic object, two tracks can create harmonic ambiguity. For example, a bass establishes one pitch, a guitar suggests another, a synth sustains a third, and a vocal implies a fourth. There may be only four sounds, but the listener has to reconcile multiple harmonic interpretations. That can create significant perceptual density. So again, counting tracks isn't enough.

19. Frequency Density Is Also Contextual

We've already discussed frequency masking. Now connect it to density. Suppose five instruments occupy the same broad frequency region, even if none is individually loud, their combined activity may create a dense spectral environment. But five instruments can also be arranged so that they reinforce one another.

The question isn't simply "how much overlap exists?" It is "what does the overlap cause the listener to perceive?" Fusion, reinforcement, masking, richness, confusion, power? The answer depends on context.

20. Density Can Create Fusion

This is important because density isn't always a problem. Layering can create a larger composite sound. Think about a synth made from several oscillators, you don't necessarily want to hear each oscillator as an independent instrument, you want one larger sonic object. Likewise, orchestration can use multiple instruments to produce one combined gesture. In those situations, density contributes to fusion. The goal isn't individual clarity, the goal is a larger perceptual object.

21. When Fusion Becomes Confusion

But the same principle can go too far. Suppose five instruments are supposed to function independently, they have different melodies, different rhythms, different articulations, but they occupy similar registers, they change at similar times, they have similar envelopes, and they are all equally prominent. The listener may struggle to separate them.

This is where density becomes a problem. Not because there are five instruments. Because there are five competing identities.

22. Identity Is the Key

Every musical element carries identity. That identity can come from:

  • Pitch
  • Rhythm
  • Timbre
  • Register
  • Articulation
  • Dynamics
  • Spatial position
  • Repetition
  • Timing

If several sounds have different identities, the listener may separate them easily. If several sounds share many of the same cues, they can become harder to distinguish. This is why arrangement decisions cannot be reduced to frequency allocation.

23. Register Is a Density Control

Suppose two instruments play different melodies, put them in separate registers, the listener may find them easier to follow. Now move them into the same register, the same melodies can become more difficult to distinguish. Nothing about their notation changed, you changed where their information lives. Register is therefore one of the ways arrangement controls perceptual density.

24. But Don't Treat Register as a Rule

Again, we don't want a recipe like "always separate instruments by an octave." That's not how music works. Sometimes the same register creates fusion, sometimes it creates competition, sometimes a crowded register is exactly what makes the section powerful. The question is always: what relationship do I want the listener to perceive?

25. Rhythmic Density

Now consider rhythm. Four instruments can all play the same rhythm, that may create a powerful unified groove. Or four instruments can each play different rhythms, now the listener receives multiple temporal streams. The latter is generally more perceptually demanding. Again, this isn't inherently bad, complex rhythmic music can be incredibly compelling. But complexity needs organisation.

26. Stable Rhythm Can Support Complex Rhythm

Imagine a bass playing a simple repeating pattern, a kick holding a simple pulse, a vocal delivering a complex phrase, a guitar playing a syncopated rhythm, and percussion adding detailed subdivision. The simple bass and kick give the listener stable references. The vocal and guitar can then introduce complexity without the entire rhythm becoming equally unstable. This is hierarchy through time.

27. Why Everything Moving at Once Feels Bigger, Until It Doesn't

There's a useful threshold here. Adding movement can make a production feel energetic, but if every element is moving constantly, movement loses its contrast. If everything changes, change itself becomes less meaningful.

That's a profound production principle. A cymbal crash is exciting partly because it is a change, if something is crashing continuously, the crash loses its special status. A fill is exciting because it interrupts a pattern, if every bar contains a fill, the fill becomes the pattern.

28. Contrast Creates Meaning

This is why:

  • Loud needs quiet
  • Dense needs sparse
  • Busy needs simple
  • Change needs stability
  • Foreground needs background
  • Movement needs stillness

These aren't rigid psychoacoustic laws, they're useful musical relationships. The listener perceives differences. If everything has the same perceptual intensity, hierarchy disappears.

29. A Listening Experiment: The Chorus

Take one of your own mixes, listen to the chorus. Write down every element that enters when the chorus begins. Now ask: which one is the most important? Then: which three are supporting it? Then: which elements could disappear without damaging the musical idea?

Now mute one, listen. Mute another, listen. Don't ask "does the mix sound fuller?" Ask "can I hear the important thing more clearly?" That's the experiment.

30. Density and Loudness

Density is often confused with loudness. A dense mix isn't necessarily loud, a quiet arrangement can be extremely dense, a loud arrangement can be sparse. However, high density can make loudness feel more constant because there are fewer moments of reduced energy. That reduces contrast, and reduced contrast can make a production feel smaller or flatter even if its average level is high.

31. Why Dynamic Contrast Matters

Imagine a verse that is moderately sparse, then a chorus arrives with a moderate increase in density. The listener experiences change. Now imagine a verse that's already extremely dense, the chorus adds even more layers, there is still an increase, but the perceptual contrast may be much smaller.

This is why a chorus can have more tracks than a verse and still feel less dramatic than expected. The verse already consumed much of the available density range.

32. The Arrangement Has a Density Budget

Think of this as a conceptual tool, not a literal measurement. Every section has a certain amount of information the listener can comfortably organise. You can spend that budget on:

  • Rhythm
  • Harmony
  • Melody
  • Instrumentation
  • Register
  • Articulation
  • Dynamics
  • Timbral change

If you spend heavily in every category simultaneously, the section becomes extremely information-rich. If you simplify one category, another can become more prominent. For example: a simple drum pattern can support a complex vocal, a simple vocal phrase can support a complex instrumental arrangement, a stable bass can support harmonic movement above it.

33. Production Can Reduce Density Without Removing the Part

This is where production becomes powerful. Suppose a guitar part is too busy, you don't necessarily have to delete it, you can change:

  • Articulation
  • Note length
  • Envelope
  • Dynamics
  • Transient intensity
  • Performance
  • Register
  • Timing

The part remains, but its perceptual demand decreases. That's a very different kind of production decision from simply muting the track.

34. Compression Can Increase Perceptual Density

Compression can sometimes make a sound more continuously present. A highly dynamic part may have moments where it recedes, compressing it can make its information more consistently available, that can be useful. But if many tracks are treated this way, the entire production can become continuously active, nothing recedes, nothing breathes, everything occupies attention all the time. This is one reason over-compression can contribute to perceived density.

35. Sustain Is Information

Consider a guitar chord, if it decays naturally, the next chord arrives after some of the previous energy has disappeared. If you increase sustain dramatically, the old chord remains while the new chord arrives, now the listener is processing both. Repeat this across many instruments, you can create a constantly overlapping sonic environment.

Again, the problem isn't necessarily too many sounds, it can be too much information remaining active.

36. Arrangement Is About Information Lifetimes

This is a useful way to think about production. Every sound has a lifetime: it starts, it changes, it sustains, it decays, it stops. If many sounds have long lifetimes, they overlap. If many sounds have short lifetimes, information turns over quickly. Both can work, but they create very different perceptual environments. This connects temporal masking, density and arrangement.

37. The Importance of Decay

Decay is often overlooked because we focus on attack, but decay determines how long an event continues to occupy the perceptual field. A short percussion hit disappears quickly, a sustained pad continues, a long guitar sustain overlaps the next phrase, a compressed vocal remains consistently present. These are all different ways of controlling information persistence.

38. Why Shortening a Sound Can Create Space

Imagine a guitar part that feels like it is getting in the way of the vocal. Instead of EQing the guitar, shorten its notes. Suddenly the guitar stops competing during the vocal phrase, the guitar is still there, but its information doesn't remain active for as long. That's a production solution to a mixing problem.

39. Density and Stereo Width

Stereo can help organise information, but width doesn't automatically solve density. Imagine ten instruments spread across the stereo field, they may be physically separated, yet if all ten are changing simultaneously, the arrangement can still feel extremely busy. Likewise, a mono production can feel surprisingly spacious if its information is sparse and hierarchical. Spatial separation is one cue, it isn't the whole solution.

40. Density and Depth

The same applies to depth. You can put one sound "far back," but if it remains extremely active and rhythmically busy, it can still demand attention. Conversely, a relatively dry sound can remain in the background if it has low perceptual priority. Depth is therefore not simply distance, it's hierarchy.

41. The Listener Doesn't Read Your Track List

This is worth remembering. You may know track 17 is just a subtle texture, the listener doesn't know that. If track 17 introduces a new rhythm exactly when the vocal begins, it may become perceptually important. The listener hears the information, not your intention. That's why mixing decisions should always return to the actual listening experience.

42. The Problem of "Everything Is Important"

One of the easiest ways to create density is to make every element equally important. Every track gets:

  • Compression
  • Saturation
  • Enhancement
  • Width
  • Presence
  • Automation

Everything is polished, everything is audible, everything is exciting. And then the production becomes strangely unexciting. Because there is no hierarchy.

43. Importance Needs Contrast

A foreground element becomes foreground partly because something else is background. If everything is foreground, there is no foreground. This is why subtractive mixing is so powerful.

Turn down the supporting guitar, the vocal becomes more important. Reduce the percussion during the phrase, the vocal becomes more important. Simplify the bass rhythm, the vocal's rhythm becomes more noticeable. Remove a synth, the guitar may suddenly become emotionally important. You haven't necessarily improved individual sounds, you've improved relationships.

44. Mixing Is Relational

This is one of the central ideas of FREQ. A mix isn't a collection of independently optimised tracks, it is a perceptual system. A vocal can sound perfect alone and wrong in context. A guitar can sound thin alone and perfect in context. A bass can sound weak alone and enormous in the mix. A drum can sound unimpressive alone and perfectly define the groove. So don't optimise sounds independently, optimise their relationships.

45. A Density Diagnostic

When a mix feels crowded, don't immediately ask "which plugin should I use?" Ask:

  1. How many independent events are happening?
  2. How many are changing simultaneously?
  3. How many have strong transients?
  4. How many remain sustained?
  5. How many occupy similar registers?
  6. How many have independent rhythms?
  7. How many are competing for foreground status?
  8. Is there anything stable for the listener to use as a reference?

Those questions can reveal the problem much faster than staring at an analyser.

46. Listening Experiment: Remove the Rhythm

Take a dense section, mute the percussion, listen. Now mute the guitars, listen. Now mute the synths, listen. Notice which removal creates the greatest perceptual relief. That track may not be "bad," it may simply be contributing a lot of information. Now ask whether that information is necessary.

47. Listening Experiment: Remove Sustains

Find a dense arrangement with many sustained sounds. Shorten the decay or note length of one element, listen. Then another, listen. You may find that the mix becomes clearer without any EQ change. That's because you reduced the amount of information remaining active at once.

48. Listening Experiment: Stable Reference

Choose one element to remain extremely consistent through a section, for example a repeating bass figure. Now allow another element to become more complex, listen. Then remove the stable element, listen again. The complex part may become harder to follow. This demonstrates why stable references can support complexity.

49. The Arrangement Can Breathe

Think of a song as a conversation. If everyone speaks simultaneously, adding more speakers doesn't make the conversation richer, it makes it harder to follow. Music can work the same way. A phrase can answer another phrase, a percussion hit can leave room for the vocal, a guitar can fill the gap left by the vocal, a synth can appear after the chord rather than underneath every moment. This is arrangement as conversation.

50. Density Creates Drama When Controlled

We shouldn't conclude "keep everything sparse," that would be just another rigid recipe. Dense music can be incredibly powerful. The important thing is that density itself becomes a musical variable, you can build toward it, reduce it, hold it, suddenly increase it, then remove it. A dense section becomes more meaningful when the listener has experienced lower-density sections. Contrast gives density emotional significance.

51. The Biggest Chorus May Be the Simplest

This is a counterintuitive production idea. A chorus doesn't necessarily become bigger by adding more parts, it can become bigger by making the important parts more dominant. Sometimes the biggest chorus has:

  • Fewer notes
  • Fewer rhythms
  • Longer sounds
  • Stronger unison
  • More stable references
  • Fewer competing events

The production feels larger because the listener can perceive the main idea more completely.

52. Density and Emotion

This isn't merely technical, perceptual density changes emotional experience. Sparse arrangements can feel intimate, exposed, fragile, spacious. Dense arrangements can feel overwhelming, euphoric, aggressive, chaotic, monumental. The same amount of information can produce different emotions depending on how it's organised. So density is not a problem to eliminate, it is a parameter to compose.

53. The Deeper Principle

We're now getting close to the larger philosophy. The listener does not experience tracks, the listener experiences relationships. They don't hear your 48-channel session. They hear objects, events, patterns, contrasts, hierarchy, movement, stability. Your job is not to make all 48 tracks audible. Your job is to make the intended relationships perceptible.

54. Why "More" Often Feels Smaller

We can finally answer the question from the beginning. Why can adding more sounds make a mix feel smaller? Because additional information can:

  1. Increase competition
  2. Reduce contrast
  3. Reduce hierarchy
  4. Increase temporal density
  5. Increase spectral density
  6. Reduce stable references
  7. Increase overlapping sustain
  8. Create more simultaneous changes
  9. Blur individual identities
  10. Increase listener processing load

The physical sound has increased, but perceptual distinction can decrease. And perceptual distinction is what makes a production feel clear and large.

55. The FREQ Perspective

This is where the broader FREQ framework becomes useful. Composition creates information, you decide what musical ideas exist. Arrangement organizes information, you decide which ideas coexist. Production shapes information, you decide how those ideas arrive and how long they remain perceptually active. Mixing balances information, you establish hierarchy and relationships. Mastering prepares the final object. And then the listener perceives the result.

The final stage determines whether all your information actually survived as meaningful information.

56. The Question to Take Into Your Next Mix

When you add another layer, don't just ask "does this sound good?" Ask "what information does this add?" Then ask "when does that information matter?" And finally, "what becomes harder to hear because I added it?" That last question is incredibly powerful, because every addition has an opportunity cost, something else may become less distinct.

57. Final Listening Exercise

Take one of your densest sections. Don't touch EQ, don't touch compression, don't touch reverb, don't touch stereo width. Instead, make three versions:

  • Version A: everything.
  • Version B: remove approximately 20% of the least important information.
  • Version C: remove another 20%.

Now level-match them as reasonably as possible. Listen. Which version feels larger, clearer, more emotional, more focused, more energetic? Don't assume the densest version wins. Listen.

58. What to Remember

Perceptual density isn't about the number of tracks, it is about how much information is being presented to the listener and how much of that information competes for attention. Remember:

  • More tracks don't automatically mean a bigger production.
  • More frequency content doesn't automatically mean more richness.
  • More movement doesn't automatically mean more excitement.
  • More layers don't automatically mean more depth.

Sometimes the opposite happens. A production becomes larger when the listener can clearly perceive its important information. And sometimes the way to achieve that is not to add anything, it's to remove something.

Closing

A great arrangement isn't necessarily the one with the most ideas, it's the one where the listener can understand the ideas you chose to present. A great production isn't necessarily the one with the most layers, it's the one where each layer contributes something perceptually meaningful. And a great mix isn't necessarily the one where every track sounds impressive, it's the one where the listener understands what matters now. That's the central idea of perceptual density.

The listener has limited attention. So your job isn't to give them less music, your job is to organise more music intelligently. Because when everything happens at once, the listener doesn't necessarily hear more, they may hear less. And when you create hierarchy, contrast and stable reference points, something remarkable happens: the same number of sounds can suddenly feel bigger.

In the next episode, we'll take the idea of hierarchy further. We'll look at what happens when several parts are changing at once, and how the arrangement can tell the listener which change matters most.

Practical takeaways

  1. Track count is not perceptual density.
  2. Density depends on how much information is active and changing at once.
  3. Simultaneous changes can increase perceptual load.
  4. Stable elements provide reference points.
  5. Repetition can make later complexity easier to understand.
  6. Sustained sounds continue occupying perceptual attention.
  7. Shortening an element can create space without EQ.
  8. Compression can increase continuous perceptual presence.
  9. Stereo width doesn't automatically solve density.
  10. Depth isn't simply distance; hierarchy matters.
  11. Layering can intentionally create fusion and larger composite objects.
  12. Density is not inherently bad; it can create power, excitement and scale.
  13. Contrast makes density meaningful.
  14. Removing information can make important information feel larger.
  15. The key diagnostic question is: what information does this element add, and what becomes harder to perceive because it is there?

Episode summary

Why More Sounds Can Make a Mix Feel Smaller argues that perceptual density, not track count, is what determines whether a mix feels clear and large or crowded and small. Adding tracks doesn't automatically add perceptual size; what matters is how much information is competing for the listener's attention at the same moment.

The episode draws on real FREQ concepts including track density (perceptual weight), listener processing load, repetition as a stable reference, and contrast, to build a practical diagnostic: when a mix feels crowded, ask how many things are changing simultaneously and whether the listener has anything stable to hold onto, before reaching for a plugin.

The takeaway is that subtraction is often the tool that makes a mix feel bigger: removing competing information can make the important information more perceptually prominent than adding processing ever could.
