Episode purpose

Episode 37 covered what to deliver. This episode opens a new arc about the relationship around that delivery, arguing that client communication is a professional skill in its own right, not a soft add-on to the technical work of mixing.

Script

Opening

A mixing client is trusting you with something they care about. Sometimes they know exactly what they want. Sometimes they have no idea how to describe what they're hearing. Sometimes they need to ask ten questions before they feel comfortable. Being a good mixer is not only about making good mixes. It's also about making the person on the other side of the project feel that their music is in safe hands.

The FREQ question: what is this client actually trying to tell me, and am I translating it accurately instead of correcting it?

1. Every Client Needs Some Hand-Holding

Some clients need very little, they send files, references, notes and a deadline, and they know exactly what they're listening for. Others need much more involvement, they may want to understand every step, need help preparing files, or need several conversations before approving a mix. None of this makes them bad clients, they're simply at different points in their experience. You were a beginner once too.

2. Always Be Patient

A client may ask a question that seems obvious to you. Answer it. They may use the wrong technical term, understand what they're trying to communicate. You've spent years learning how audio works, they may have spent those years writing songs, performing or producing. The fact that something is obvious to you is not a reason for someone else to feel uncomfortable asking about it.

3. Make the Client Feel Safe Asking Questions

A client who's afraid of looking inexperienced may stop communicating, and that's when misunderstandings begin. If someone thinks they should already know what "headroom" means, they may nod instead of asking. If they don't understand why you changed something, they may approve it without actually liking it. Make it clear that questions are welcome, the more comfortable a client is communicating honestly, the easier it becomes to make the right decisions for the song.

4. Assure Them of Your Skills, Without Exaggerating

Clients are often nervous when they hand over a song. Part of your responsibility is to give them confidence, tell them what you understand about the project, what you're going to do, and when they can expect to hear something. Reassurance doesn't require a sales pitch, you need to demonstrate that you understand the work and have a clear plan for it.

5. Never Exaggerate

Confidence and exaggeration are not the same thing. Don't promise something you can't guarantee. Don't tell a client you can make their recording sound exactly like another record, and don't promise a result before you've heard the material. Professional confidence comes from knowing what you can do and being honest about what you can't.

6. Bite Only What You Can Chew

One of the easiest ways to damage a client relationship is to promise more than you can deliver. If a project requires more time than expected, don't pretend it will still be finished in an unrealistic window. A smaller promise that you consistently keep is more valuable than a huge promise that repeatedly slips.

7. When You Are Running Late, Communicate Early

Sometimes things take longer than expected. Whatever the reason, don't disappear. If you know the original delivery time is no longer realistic, tell the client as soon as you know, be honest about the situation, and give them a realistic new expectation. That's very different from saying nothing until the deadline has passed.

8. Being Honest Does Not Mean Being Defensive

If something goes wrong, explain what happened without making excuses. There's a difference between context and justification. The client usually doesn't need a long story about your day, they need to know what happened, what it means for their project, and what happens next. Keep the communication calm and direct.

9. Do Not Hide Problems Until They Become Bigger

If you discover that something important is missing, say so. If the supplied files have a problem, say so. If a requested change creates a problem elsewhere, explain the trade-off. Small problems are usually manageable. Hidden problems become surprises, and surprises damage trust much faster than honest conversations do.

10. Learn to Translate, Not Correct

Clients often describe sound in everyday language, "the vocal needs to be bigger," "the guitar is eating the singer," "the chorus needs to open up," "the drums don't feel powerful." Don't respond by correcting their terminology. Listen to what they're actually trying to communicate, then investigate the technical reason behind that perception. The client describes the experience. You bring the technical knowledge needed to act on it.

11. This Is the Same Skill As Riding a Vocal

Episode 28 argued that a vocal ride effectively says "listen here" to the listener, deciding what deserves attention. Translating client feedback works the same way, "the vocal needs to be bigger" might genuinely mean level, but it might also mean register, from Episode 27, or depth, from Episode 30, or a masking problem, from Episode 29. The client hands you the symptom, your job is diagnosing the actual cause.

12. Respect Their Taste

You may know more about mixing than your client, that doesn't mean your taste automatically outranks theirs. They may want a vocal brighter than you'd normally choose, or more low end, or an effect you wouldn't personally use. If the choice is intentional and works for the artistic direction, it doesn't need to match your personal preference. Your expertise should help the client achieve their vision, not replace it with yours.

13. Kindness Is a Professional Skill

Mixing can involve a lot of vulnerability. A client is giving you something they may have spent months or years creating. Be kind. You don't lose authority by being patient. You don't become less professional by being understanding. You become someone people want to work with again.

14. Real Beats Impressive

The best client communication doesn't sound like a sales script. It sounds like a person who knows their work and is comfortable being honest, "I can do that," "I need to hear the files first," "I need another day," "I don't think we should change that because it's part of what makes the performance work." Those statements build more trust than exaggerated promises ever will.

15. What to Remember

Every client is at a different point in their experience, and none of them owe you familiarity with technical terminology. A client's plain-language feedback, "the vocal needs to be bigger," is information to translate into a technical diagnosis, not terminology to correct. Honesty about timelines, problems and limitations builds trust faster than exaggerated confidence or hidden difficulties. Respecting a client's intentional taste, even when it differs from your own preference, keeps your expertise in service of their vision. The technical quality of a mix gets you noticed, but how you handle the person behind it is what makes them trust you with the next one.

Closing

You don't need to pretend everything is always perfect. You need to show that you can handle the situation when it's not. Be patient, be honest, respect the vision you're serving, and be kind.

Practical takeaways

  1. Clients are at different points in their experience with mixing; needing extra explanation doesn't make someone a difficult client.
  2. Making a client feel safe asking questions prevents silent misunderstandings that surface much later as dissatisfaction.
  3. Confidence should come from honesty about capability, not exaggerated promises about results you haven't yet heard the material for.
  4. Communicating a delay as soon as you know about it preserves trust far better than staying silent until a deadline passes.
  5. Small, disclosed problems stay manageable; hidden problems become surprises that damage trust much faster.
  6. Plain-language client feedback like "the vocal needs to be bigger" is information to diagnose technically, not terminology to correct.
  7. The same feedback phrase can point to several different technical causes, level, register, depth or masking, and diagnosing correctly is the job.
  8. Respecting a client's intentional artistic choices matters even when they differ from a mixer's personal preference.
  9. Real, direct communication builds more trust over time than a polished, sales-oriented pitch.
  10. Technical mix quality gets a mixer noticed; how they handle the client relationship is what brings the client back.

Episode summary

How to Talk to a Mixing Client treats client communication as a professional skill equal in importance to technical mixing ability. Patience with beginners, honesty about timelines and limitations, and translating plain-language feedback into technical diagnosis are shown to matter as much as any EQ decision covered earlier in the series.

The episode connects directly to Episode 28's vocal-riding principle, treating client feedback interpretation as the same "what is this actually asking for" skill applied to a conversation instead of a fader, and shows how a single phrase like "the vocal needs to be bigger" could point to register, depth, masking or simple level, all covered in earlier episodes.

The throughline: the technical quality of a mix gets a mixer noticed, but how they handle the person behind the project, with patience, honesty and respect for their taste, is what makes a client come back.
