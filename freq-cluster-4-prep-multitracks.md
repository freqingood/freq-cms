## The mix is won or lost before you upload

Ask any seasoned producer what separates a smooth mix from a painful one, and very few will start with plugins or technique. They will start with preparation.

**A clean, correctly exported set of multitrack files makes every later decision easier; a messy one sabotages the mix before it begins.** Tracks that start at different points, sit at different sample rates, or arrive already clipping will fight you at every step, and no amount of mixing skill fully recovers from a bad export.

This is the professional pre-flight checklist. Get this right and your session opens clean, aligned, and ready, whether you are mixing it yourself in FREQ or handing it to an engineer.

## The Pre-Flight Checklist

Before anything goes into FREQ, every track in your song should pass these five checks.

### 1. Every Track Starts at the Same Point and Shares the Same Length

This is the single most important rule, and the one beginners break most often.

**Export every track from the exact same start point; the beginning of the song; even if a track is silent there.** A backing vocal that only appears in the final chorus should still be exported as a full-length file that begins at bar one, with silence up to its entry.

Why: if tracks start at different points, they will not line up when loaded, and your perfectly recorded performance will play back out of time. Exporting everything from the same zero point, at the same length, guarantees the session lines up automatically. This is often called consolidating or bouncing from the start of the timeline.

### 2. Every Track Shares the Same Sample Rate

All your files must be at one sample rate. Mixing a 44.1 kHz track with a 48 kHz track in the same session causes timing and pitch problems or forces conversion that can degrade quality.

Pick one sample rate for the whole project at the start, and export everything at it. The next section covers how to choose.

### 3. Every Track Shares the Same Bit Depth

Bit depth controls the resolution and dynamic range of your audio. Export all tracks at the same bit depth.

- **24-bit** is the professional standard for mixing. It gives ample dynamic range and headroom, and it is the right default for almost everyone.
- **32-bit float**, if your tool offers it, is effectively clip-proof on export and a strong choice for delivery into a mix.
- **Avoid 16-bit** for mixing. It is a delivery format for finished masters, not a working format; exporting your tracks at 16-bit throws away resolution you want to keep while mixing.

### 4. No Clipping, Unless It Is Deliberate

A clipped track has distortion baked permanently into the file. You cannot remove it later.

**Leave headroom on every track.** Aim for peaks comfortably below 0 dBFS; peaks landing around -6 dBFS are a safe, common target. This gives the mix room to breathe and prevents the harsh, crunchy artefacts of digital clipping.

The exception is intent. If you deliberately drove a drum bus into distortion as a creative choice and you want that sound, keep it; that is a decision, not an accident. The rule is simply this: clipping should only ever be something you chose, never something that happened to you.

### 5. Clean, Labelled, and Free of Junk

Before export, tidy the session:

- Delete muted takes, unused tracks, and dead audio you do not want in the mix
- Give every track a clear, descriptive name; "Lead Vox," "Kick In," "Bass DI," not "Audio 12"
- Remove or print any processing you have committed to, and leave dry anything you want to shape later

A clean export is a gift to whoever mixes it next, even if that person is you in a week.

## Choosing Your Sample Rate: 44.1 kHz or 48 kHz

This decision confuses people endlessly, but the rule is simple once you know what your song is for.

**The question to ask: will this audio live on its own as music, or will it live with picture?**

### Use 44.1 kHz for music releases

44.1 kHz is the long-standing standard for music distribution. Choose it when your song is headed for:

- A single or album on Spotify, Apple Music, or any music streaming platform
- Digital download stores and CDs
- A sample pack for other producers

**Real example:** You are releasing an independent single to Spotify and Apple Music. Export and mix the whole project at 44.1 kHz.

### Use 48 kHz for anything with video

48 kHz is the standard for film, television, and online video. Choose it when your audio will be paired with picture:

- A music video where the audio is embedded in the video file
- A track made for a YouTube video, vlog, or other content
- A film or short-film score
- A television or social media advertisement
- A video podcast
- Game audio

**Real example:** You are scoring a short film, or producing the music bed for a brand's YouTube ad. Export and mix at 48 kHz, because the audio will be married to video.

### The one rule that matters most

Whatever you choose, **keep every track in the project at that same rate.** Decide at the start based on the destination, and do not mix rates within a single song. When in doubt and the song is music first, 44.1 kHz is the safe default.

## Know Your Track Types Before You Assign Depth

FREQ's depth assignment is more capable than a simple front-to-back slider, and a pro prepares for it. Alongside the standard depth lanes, FREQ provides dedicated lanes for specific track roles:

- **Reverb lanes** for reverb returns and wet-only reverb tracks
- **Parallel compression lanes** for parallel compression prints and sends
- **Room track lanes** for room mics, overheads, and ambience captures
- **Null lanes** for tracks that need no treatment at all; fully finished or printed elements you want left completely untouched

**Prepare with these in mind.** As you export, know the role of every file. Tag your reverb returns, your parallel compression prints, your room mics, and your already-finished tracks. When you reach the Separation stage in FREQ, you assign each to the correct lane in seconds rather than puzzling over what each file is.

This is the difference between a producer who dumps thirty unlabelled files and one who hands over a session that practically assigns itself. Knowing which tracks want treatment, which want a specific kind of treatment, and which want none at all is a core part of professional prep.

## How to Upload to FREQ

FREQ gives you two ways to bring your prepared files in:

- **As a single zip file:** Compress your whole folder of multitrack files into one zip and upload it in a single action. This is the fastest route for a full session and keeps everything together.
- **Individually:** Upload tracks one by one when you want to add or replace specific files, or build a session up piece by piece.

For a complete song, the zip route is usually quickest. For revisions or adding a late overdub, individual upload is convenient.

## The Rough Mix: Bring Your Intent With You

A rough mix is your reference; the version that captures what you were going for before detailed mixing begins. FREQ supports two approaches:

- **Create a rough mix inside FREQ.** Use the platform's tools, including Optimize Balance for a fast calibrated starting point, to build a quick reference balance directly in your session.
- **Upload a rough mix you already have.** If you made a reference mix in your recording tool, bring it in and assign it as your rough mix, so you can A/B your detailed work against the feel you originally intended.

Either way, having a rough mix matters. It anchors your decisions to your original vision and gives you something to compare against when your ears start to drift. A producer who works without a reference is mixing blind.

## The Expert Prep Checklist

Run through this before every upload:

1. All tracks exported from the same start point, same length
2. All tracks at the same sample rate (44.1 kHz for music, 48 kHz for video)
3. All tracks at the same bit depth (24-bit standard, or 32-bit float)
4. No clipping on any track, unless it is a deliberate creative choice
5. Session cleaned: junk removed, every track clearly named
6. Track roles identified: reverb returns, parallel compression, room mics, and no-treatment tracks all known
7. Files ready to upload as a zip or individually
8. A rough mix prepared, either to build in FREQ or to upload as a reference

Tick all eight and you are preparing your multitrack files like a professional.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How do I prepare my tracks for mixing?**  
Export every track from the same start point at the same length, sample rate, and bit depth, with headroom so nothing clips. Clean the session, name every track clearly, and identify each track's role. In FREQ you can then upload the whole set as a zip or individually and assign each to the correct depth lane.

**Should I export at 44.1 kHz or 48 kHz?**  
Use 44.1 kHz for music releases such as singles and albums on streaming platforms, CDs, and download stores. Use 48 kHz when the audio will live with video, including music videos, YouTube content, film scores, advertisements, and games. Keep every track in the project at the same rate.

**What bit depth should I use for mixing?**  
Use 24-bit as the standard for mixing, or 32-bit float if your tool supports it. Both give the dynamic range and headroom a mix needs. Avoid 16-bit for working files; it is a finished-master delivery format, not a mixing format.

**Why do my tracks need to start at the same point?**  
If tracks start at different points, they will not line up when loaded and the song will play back out of time. Exporting every track from the beginning of the song, including any silence before it enters, guarantees the session aligns automatically.

**How much headroom should I leave on my tracks?**  
Leave each track peaking comfortably below 0 dBFS; peaks around -6 dBFS are a common, safe target. This prevents clipping and gives the mix room to breathe. Clipping should only ever appear if you deliberately chose it as a creative effect.

**Should I upload my tracks as a zip or one by one?**  
For a full song, uploading a single zip of all your multitrack files is fastest and keeps the set together. Upload individually when you are adding, replacing, or revising specific tracks. FREQ supports both.

**Do I need a rough mix before I start?**  
A rough mix is strongly recommended because it captures your original intent and gives you a reference to compare against. You can build one inside FREQ using Optimize Balance for a fast starting balance, or upload a reference mix you already made.

**What are null lanes in FREQ?**  
Null lanes are for tracks that need no treatment at all, such as fully finished or printed elements you want left completely untouched. Assigning them to a null lane keeps them clean while the rest of your mix is processed. FREQ also provides dedicated lanes for reverb returns, parallel compression, and room tracks.
