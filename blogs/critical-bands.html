What Are Critical Bands? The Foundation of Frequency Masking

Published Aug 4, 2026 · Updated Aug 11, 2026

Every mixing engineer eventually discovers that some instruments seem to fight each other no matter how carefully they're balanced. You lower one instrument, boost another, add EQ, and yet the mix still feels crowded.

The problem often isn't the mix. It's how human hearing works .

Before understanding frequency masking, upward spread of masking, or why certain EQ moves are so effective, it's worth understanding one of the most important ideas in psychoacoustics: critical bands .

Your Ear Is Not a Spectrum Analyzer

It's easy to imagine hearing as a perfectly continuous line from 20 Hz to 20 kHz. If that were true, every frequency would be analysed independently.

Instead, the cochlea acts like a bank of overlapping biological filters, a mechanism described in the auditory-filter and loudness-model research literature (Moore, Development and Current Status of the Cambridge Loudness Models). As sound travels through the inner ear, different regions of the basilar membrane respond most strongly to different frequencies. The brain receives information from these regions rather than from every individual frequency separately.

These overlapping regions are known as critical bands . Humans perceive roughly twenty-four of them across the audible spectrum, a widely used approximation known as the Bark scale (Moore, Cambridge Loudness Models); more recent auditory-filter models (ERB, equivalent rectangular bandwidth) describe this as a continuous, level-dependent function rather than a fixed biological count.

Rather than processing thousands of frequencies individually, the auditory system groups nearby frequencies into these bands before your brain interprets the sound.

Why Critical Bands Exist

Imagine trying to understand an orchestra by listening to every vibrating air molecule independently. Your brain would be overwhelmed.

Instead, the auditory system organises information into manageable frequency regions. This allows us to identify voices, instruments, and musical notes remarkably quickly.

Critical bands are therefore not a limitation. They're an efficient feature of human hearing.

The trade-off is that sounds occupying the same band begin competing for perception.

Where Frequency Masking Comes From

If two sounds occupy the same critical band at the same time, the louder sound raises the hearing threshold for the quieter one. The quieter sound becomes harder to perceive even though it is still physically present.

This phenomenon is called frequency masking .

Notice what causes the masking. It is not because two frequencies happen to be numerically close together. It is because the auditory system is attempting to process both sounds inside the same biological filter.

This is why two instruments separated by only a small amount may remain perfectly clear in one part of the spectrum while becoming muddy in another. Critical bands become progressively wider as frequency increases (Moore, Cambridge Loudness Models), so the ear does not treat every octave equally.

Why Equal EQ Moves Don't Sound Equal

Many engineers assume that reducing 3 dB at one frequency should have roughly the same audible effect anywhere in the spectrum.

Human hearing doesn't work that way.

Because critical bands vary in width across the audible range, identical numerical EQ moves can produce very different perceptual results.

This explains why small cuts around one frequency can dramatically improve clarity while much larger adjustments elsewhere barely change the mix.

Mixing is ultimately about perception rather than mathematics.

Arrangement Often Beats EQ

Suppose two guitars occupy nearly the same frequency region with similar tone and timing. Adding more EQ may provide only limited improvement because both instruments continue sharing the same critical bands.

Changing the arrangement, altering the voicing, selecting a different instrument, or shifting the octave often produces a much larger improvement than another equaliser plugin.

This is one reason experienced mix engineers often solve problems before reaching for processing.

Critical Bands Explain Upward Spread of Masking

Critical bands also help explain why excessive low-frequency energy can reduce clarity much higher in the spectrum.

The mechanics of the cochlea mean that powerful low-frequency energy influences neighbouring regions more than the reverse.

This phenomenon is known as upward spread of masking.

Understanding critical bands first makes upward spread much easier to understand because both phenomena originate from the same biological hearing system.

What This Means for Mixing

Thinking in critical bands changes the questions you ask while mixing.

Instead of asking,

"What frequency should I boost?"

you begin asking,

"Which instruments are competing inside the same critical band?"

That shift in thinking often leads to cleaner arrangements, gentler EQ moves, and mixes that translate more naturally across different playback systems.

Final Thoughts

Critical bands are one of the fundamental building blocks of psychoacoustics. They explain why masking exists, why some EQ moves work better than others, and why arrangement frequently solves problems more effectively than processing.

Once you understand how the ear groups frequencies together, many mixing decisions become easier because they begin aligning with the biology of hearing instead of fighting against it.
