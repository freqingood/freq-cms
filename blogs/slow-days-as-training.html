What Should You Do on Days When You Have No Mixing Work?

Published Aug 9, 2026 · Updated Aug 11, 2026

A busy mixing schedule gives you plenty of opportunities to practice.

A slow schedule gives you something different: freedom.

When there is no client deadline, no revision waiting, and no song that needs to be delivered today, you can use the time to work on the part of mixing that rarely gets enough attention during paid projects.

Training.

Think of it the way an athlete approaches their craft. Competition is where performance matters. Training is where you deliberately develop abilities that you may need later.

A mixing project is not always the best place to experiment.

A quiet day is.

Client Work Is Not Always the Place to Learn

When someone has trusted you with their song, your job is to serve the recording.

That does not mean you should never experiment. Good mixing requires exploration.

But there is a difference between trying something because the song needs it and trying something because you have never used it before.

A client project should not become your laboratory every time you install a new plugin.

That is what slow days are for.

You can experiment without a deadline hanging over the decision.

You can make something terrible.

You can try the wrong approach.

You can push a processor far beyond where you would normally use it.

And nobody has to receive the result.

Try the Plugins You Never Use

Most mixers eventually develop a small collection of tools they reach for repeatedly.

That is efficient.

It can also become limiting.

A slow day is a good opportunity to open the plugins sitting in your folder that you bought, downloaded, or installed months ago but never properly learned.

Do not just read the manual.

Put the plugin on a real sound.

Listen to what it actually does.

Push it too far.

Then bring it back.

Try it on a source where you would normally never use it.

You are not necessarily looking for a new favourite plugin.

You are building familiarity.

Try a Different Way of Solving the Same Problem

Skill development does not always require learning another tool.

Sometimes the better exercise is solving a familiar problem differently.

If you normally fix a harsh vocal with EQ, try approaching the problem with dynamics.

If you normally create depth with reverb, try changing level, timing, or frequency relationships.

If you normally make the chorus bigger by making things louder, try creating contrast somewhere else.

The point is not that one approach is superior.

The point is to expand the number of solutions available to you.

When a real project eventually presents you with an unusual problem, you will have more than one familiar path to explore.

Build Experiments That You Can Actually Hear

You do not need a complicated training program.

Take one sound.

Make one change.

Listen.

Try another approach.

Compare them.

Ask yourself what changed and why.

For example, take a vocal and create several different versions:

  • one using mostly EQ
  • one using dynamics
  • one using saturation
  • one using level and arrangement relationships

Then listen without looking at the processing.

Which one feels clearer?

Which one feels more forward?

Which one preserves the character of the performance?

You are training your ability to connect processing decisions with perception.

Use Your Own Old Mixes as Training Material

Old projects are another valuable resource on slow days.

Open something you mixed months or years ago.

Listen before looking at the session.

What would you do differently now?

What still works?

What did you over-process?

What did you leave alone that you would approach differently today?

This is one of the clearest ways to see whether your mixing judgment is actually changing.

You are comparing your current ears and experience with decisions made by an earlier version of yourself.

Do not treat the old mix as something to be embarrassed by.

Treat it as a record of what you knew at the time.

Learn Something You Cannot Immediately Use

Training does not always have to produce an immediate mixing technique.

You can spend a slow day understanding something that may become useful months later.

Learn how a compressor actually responds to an envelope.

Explore how different saturation algorithms affect harmonics.

Study why a particular EQ behaves differently from another.

Experiment with routing.

Learn a new workflow in your DAW.

The goal is to increase your understanding of the tools and systems around you.

You do not need to turn every lesson into a blog post, preset, or technique.

Sometimes knowing that something exists is enough. The deeper understanding becomes useful when a future project gives you a reason to apply it.

Make Mistakes When Nobody Is Waiting for the Mix

One of the biggest advantages of a slow day is that failure becomes cheap.

You can try an approach that sounds terrible.

You can use too much compression.

You can destroy the transient.

You can make the stereo image completely wrong.

You can process something in a way you would never deliver to a client.

Then undo it.

That experience is valuable because it gives you a reference.

It is much easier to understand the boundary of a technique after you have deliberately crossed it.

Do Not Spend the Whole Day Watching Tutorials

Learning about mixing and actually mixing are different activities.

Tutorials, interviews, manuals, and articles can give you useful information, but passive consumption has a limit.

After learning something new, put it into practice.

If you watch someone demonstrate a compressor, open your DAW and try it yourself.

If you read about a particular EQ technique, find a recording and test it.

If someone explains a different mixing workflow, try using that workflow on a song.

The goal is to turn information into experience.

Keep a Small Training Folder

A simple way to make slow days more productive is to maintain a folder of material specifically for experimentation.

It could contain:

  • old mixes
  • multitrack recordings
  • problematic sounds
  • reference tracks
  • short loops
  • sounds you want to understand better

Then you do not have to spend half the day deciding what to practice.

When there is no client work, open the folder and choose one thing to investigate.

Train What You Avoid

A particularly useful question is:

"What part of mixing do I avoid because I am not confident at it?"

That might be vocal dynamics.

Low-end control.

Depth.

Automation.

Drum balance.

Translation.

Whatever it is, a slow day gives you permission to work on it without the pressure of delivering a finished record.

The areas you avoid are often the areas where deliberate practice can have the biggest effect.

Think Like an Athlete

An athlete does not only compete.

They practice specific movements, study weaknesses, test techniques, and develop abilities that may not be needed in every competition.

A mixer can work the same way.

Client work is the performance.

Training days are where you expand your options.

The benefit may not be obvious immediately.

You might spend an afternoon learning a compressor and not use it for months.

Then a difficult vocal arrives.

You recognize the problem.

You remember the experiment.

And suddenly you have another solution.

A Slow Day Is Not a Wasted Day

Not having client work can feel uncomfortable when mixing is your business.

There is a temptation to fill the time with anything that looks productive.

But there is a better option.

Use some of that time to become better at the thing you are selling.

Try the plugin you never learned.

Solve a familiar problem differently.

Open an old mix.

Experiment without consequences.

Study something and immediately test it.

Work on the skill you normally avoid.

Then, when the next client project arrives, you are not starting from where you left off.

You have added another tool, another reference, another experience, and another possible solution to your mixing vocabulary.

That is what training days are for.
