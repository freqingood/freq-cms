What Is Upward Spread of Masking?

If reducing a small amount of low-mid energy suddenly makes your entire mix sound clearer, you're hearing one of the most important psychoacoustic principles in mixing: upward spread of masking.

Unlike frequency masking in general, upward spread of masking explains why low frequencies have a disproportionately large effect on clarity. It's one of the main reasons professional engineers pay close attention to the low-mid region before reaching for high-frequency boosts.

How Your Ear Creates the Effect

Inside the cochlea, sound travels along a structure called the basilar membrane. High frequencies reach their peak response near the entrance of the cochlea, while lower frequencies travel farther before reaching their maximum response.

Because low-frequency vibrations spread farther along the membrane, they stimulate a broader region than high frequencies. The result is that a strong low-frequency sound raises the hearing threshold for frequencies above it much more effectively than the reverse.

This phenomenon is called upward spread of masking.

Why Your Mix Sounds Less Clear

Imagine your mix has excessive energy around 250 Hz.

You don't simply hear "too much 250 Hz." Instead, that energy makes it harder for your ears to perceive important information above it, including vocal articulation, guitar definition, snare attack, piano presence, and cymbal detail.

The mix starts to feel:

  • Muddy
  • Closed-in
  • Flat
  • Dull
  • Lacking separation

Ironically, many people respond by boosting high frequencies. While that can increase brightness, it often treats the symptom rather than the cause.

Why Small EQ Moves Can Sound Bigger Than They Measure

One of the most surprising aspects of psychoacoustics is that a small reduction around the low-mid range can produce a much larger perceived improvement than the EQ curve suggests.

That's because you're not only reducing energy. You're allowing previously masked details to become audible again.

Listeners often describe the result as:

  • More clarity
  • Better definition
  • Greater openness
  • Improved vocal presence

Those details were already in the recording. Your hearing simply couldn't separate them effectively.

Why Cutting 250 Hz Doesn't Always Work

This doesn't mean every muddy mix needs an EQ cut at 250 Hz.

Upward spread of masking explains why low-mid buildup often reduces clarity. It doesn't identify which instrument is responsible.

Sometimes the solution is:

  • Turning down the bass guitar slightly.
  • Changing a guitar arrangement.
  • Reducing reverb.
  • Moving a keyboard to a different octave.
  • Using dynamic EQ instead of static EQ.

Professional engineers don't cut frequencies because charts tell them to. They identify the source of the masking first.

Arrangement Matters More Than EQ

The cleanest mixes usually begin long before the mixing stage.

If several instruments all occupy the same low-mid range, no amount of EQ can completely solve the problem. Better sound selection, arrangement, and orchestration naturally reduce masking before processing is ever applied.

This is one reason why well-produced songs often seem easier to mix. The instruments already occupy complementary perceptual spaces.

The Practical Rule

Before boosting clarity, remove the masking that's preventing clarity.

That single mindset shift often produces more natural mixes than continually adding top-end EQ.
