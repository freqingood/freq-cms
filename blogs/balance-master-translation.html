From Balance to Finished Master: How to Make Your Mix Sound Right Everywhere

Published Jul 29, 2026 · Updated Aug 11, 2026

A great mix is one that survives mono and low volume

Most people judge a mix by how good it sounds loud, in stereo, on the headphones they made it on. That is the easiest possible test, and it hides almost every problem.

A mix that holds up in mono and at low volume will hold up everywhere. Phone speakers, laptop speakers, club systems, earbuds, car stereos; the difficult playback systems are mono or near-mono, and most listening happens quietly. If your balance works under those harder conditions, it works in the easy ones automatically.

This guide takes you from a rough balance to a finished, streaming-ready master, using the checks that separate a mix that sounds good in your room from one that sounds good in the world.

Part 1: Build Your Balance

Balance is the foundation. Before character, before mastering, the relationships between your elements have to be right.

Start From a Balance That Already Works

If you are staring at a full mixer with no idea where to begin, do not push faders at random. Run Optimize Balance first. It analyses your session and sets a calibrated starting balance, so the elements already sit together. You are then shaping a working mix instead of building one from silence.

Keep this in mind for the whole process: any time you feel lost or the balance has drifted somewhere wrong, Optimize Balance is your reset. It gives you a trustworthy baseline to return to and rebuild from.

The Core Mixer Controls

For every track and group, FREQ gives you:

  • Level: How loud the element sits in the mix
  • Pan: Left-to-right placement
  • Polarity: Flips phase to fix tracks that partly cancel each other and lose punch
  • Width: How wide the element spreads across the stereo field
  • Mute and Solo: Isolate or remove elements, individually or globally, to hear what each contributes
  • Depth selection: The front-to-back placement that gives your mix dimension

Work in passes. Get levels into a sensible relationship first. Then pan for space. Then set width for dimension. Use depth to separate elements that are fighting, rather than just turning one of them down.

Mix in Mono

This is the technique that catches the most problems, and almost no beginner uses it.

Collapsing your mix to mono forces every element to share the same space. In stereo, you can hide a crowded mix by spreading things left and right. In mono, that escape route disappears. If an element vanishes or turns to mush in mono, it is being masked; two sounds are competing for the same frequency range, a phenomenon rooted in how the ear's auditory filters group nearby frequencies together.

FREQ includes a mono check for exactly this. Switch to mono regularly while balancing. When you fix the mix so everything is still clearly audible in mono, it will sound open and detailed when you return to stereo. Mono is also how a huge share of real-world playback happens, so a mix that works in mono is a mix that survives the world.

Mix at Low Volume

Loud is seductive and dishonest. At high volume, your ears exaggerate bass and treble, so almost any mix sounds big and exciting; this is a documented feature of hearing described by equal-loudness contours, which show that the ear's frequency response changes with playback level. Mix loud and you will make decisions that fall apart the moment someone plays the song quietly.

Mix at low volume; quiet enough to hold a conversation over. At low levels your ears hear the frequency range more evenly, so the balance you set is the real one. If the vocal still sits right and the kick still has weight when the whole song is quiet, your balance is genuinely solid. Low-volume mixing also protects your ears from fatigue, which is what makes long sessions go wrong.

A simple rule: make your big decisions quiet, and only turn it up to check.

Part 2: Add Character With the Console

Once your balance is right, you can give the mix a feel.

A correct mix sounds clean. A mix with character sounds intentional, like it belongs to a particular sound and a particular time. FREQ 's Console section provides this through algorithm-driven processing; every console character is built from algorithms rather than borrowed from hardware, so it is fully digital.

The era slider moves the tonal character of your mix across different sonic periods, applied across the depth lanes you set up earlier. Pull it toward a vintage character for warmth and restraint, or toward a modern character for clarity and forward presence. Different console types give you different starting characters to shape from.

The point of this stage is identity. Your balance decided where everything sits; the console decides what the whole thing feels like.

Part 3: Master Your Song

Mastering is the final shaping pass over your whole mix, turning a finished mix into a finished record. FREQ runs the complete chain in the same session, so there is no exporting to another tool.

  • EQ: Sets the final tonal balance across the entire song
  • Dynamics: Controls the overall movement between loud and soft
  • Glue: Binds the separate elements into one cohesive whole
  • Density: Adds fullness and body to the sound
  • 3D: Enhances the front-to-back dimension of the finished master
  • Tape: Adds smoothness and harmonic character
  • Stereo width: Sets how wide the final master sits
  • Final gain: Brings the master up to its target loudness

The Quick Settings triangle lets you move quickly between broad mastering characters, so you can audition different finished feels without rebuilding the chain each time.

What mastering does, in one line: it makes your song translate; sounding full and consistent whether it plays on earbuds, a laptop, a car, or a club system.

A note on order: master after the balance and character are right, never to fix them. Mastering polishes a good mix; it cannot rescue a balance problem that should have been solved with level, depth, and mono checking.

Part 4: Hit Streaming Loudness Targets

Your master needs to be the right loudness for where people will actually hear it.

LUFS (Loudness Units Full Scale) is the standard measurement of integrated loudness, defined by the ITU-R BS.1770 recommendation. Streaming platforms use LUFS to normalise playback volume, but each platform sets its own target: Spotify and YouTube normalise around -14 LUFS, Apple Music sits closer to -16 LUFS, and other services fall in between, several also enforcing a true-peak ceiling. There is no single universal number every platform shares. If your master is much louder than a platform's target, that platform turns it down, and over-compressing to "win" on loudness just makes your song quieter and flatter than a track that respected the target.

FREQ shows a live LUFS readout as you work, so you can see where your master actually lands and master with real numbers in front of you instead of guessing.

Before you call it done, run the mono check one last time on the finished master. Confirm the song still holds together summed to mono, because that is how it will play on many phones, club systems, and public spaces. A master that sounds great in stereo and collapses in mono is not finished.

Stuck at Any Point? Use Optimize Balance

Worth repeating, because it is the feature that keeps self-mixing from becoming overwhelming.

At any stage, if the mix has drifted somewhere wrong and you cannot hear your way back, run Optimize Balance to return to a calibrated foundation. It is not only a starting tool; it is a reset button for your ears. Get back to a balance that works, then make your changes more deliberately from there.

You do not need a perfect room or a trained ear to mix your own song. You need a trustworthy starting point, the discipline to check in mono and at low volume, and the confidence to reset when you lose your way. FREQ gives you all three.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I balance a mix?
Set the relationships between elements using level, pan, width, and depth, working in passes rather than all at once. Start from a calibrated balance, check your work in mono to catch masking, and judge it at low volume so your levels are honest. In FREQ , Optimize Balance gives you the starting balance and a mono check is built in.

Why should I mix in mono?
Mono forces every element to share the same space, which exposes masking and phase problems that stereo hides. If your mix is clear and balanced in mono, it will sound open in stereo and survive mono playback systems like phone speakers and many club setups. Mixing in mono first is one of the most reliable ways to get a clean balance.

Why should I mix at low volume?
At high volume your ears exaggerate bass and treble, so everything sounds good and your balance decisions become unreliable. At low volume your ears hear the frequency range more evenly, so the balance you set is the true one. Low-volume mixing also prevents the ear fatigue that causes bad late-session decisions.

What LUFS should I master to for streaming?
LUFS is the standard measurement of integrated loudness, defined by the ITU-R BS.1770 recommendation. Streaming platforms use it to normalise playback volume, but each platform sets its own target, roughly -14 LUFS on Spotify and YouTube, around -16 LUFS on Apple Music, and various points in between elsewhere, so there is no single universal number to master to. What matters is that your master is not pushed so loud that a platform turns it down, and not so quiet that it sounds weak next to other tracks. FREQ shows a live LUFS readout so you can see where your master actually lands and adjust with real numbers instead of guessing.

How do I master a song?
Mastering applies a final shaping pass to your full mix: tonal EQ, dynamics, glue, density, stereo width, and final gain to reach target loudness. Do it after your balance and character are right, not to fix them. FREQ includes the complete mastering chain in the same session, so you can master immediately after mixing.

What is the difference between mixing and mastering?
Mixing balances and shapes the individual elements of a song into a cohesive whole. Mastering treats that finished mix as a single piece and polishes it for consistency and loudness across all playback systems. Mixing works inside the song; mastering finishes the song as a whole.

What does Optimize Balance do?
Optimize Balance analyses your session and sets a calibrated starting balance automatically, so your elements already sit together. It works as both a starting point for a new mix and a reset whenever your balance drifts wrong, which makes mixing your own music realistic even without a trained ear or a treated room.
