What Are Equal-Loudness Contours? Why Your Mix Sounds Different at Different Volumes

Published Aug 4, 2026 · Updated Aug 11, 2026

Have you ever finished a mix that sounded balanced in the studio, only to discover it became muddy, harsh or lacking bass when played somewhere else?

While different speakers certainly contribute to this problem, another factor is often overlooked. Your ears themselves respond differently depending on how loudly you're listening.

This behaviour is described by one of the most important discoveries in psychoacoustics: equal-loudness contours .

Your Ears Do Not Hear Every Frequency Equally

It might seem logical that doubling the volume would make every frequency equally louder. Human hearing doesn't work that way.

Our ears are naturally most sensitive between approximately 2 kHz and 5 kHz , the frequency range where speech intelligibility is most important.

Low bass and the highest treble require significantly more acoustic energy before we perceive them as equally loud.

In other words, two sounds measured at exactly the same sound pressure level may not sound equally loud to a listener.

What Equal-Loudness Contours Show

Equal-loudness contours map the combinations of frequency and sound pressure that listeners perceive as equally loud.

At low listening levels, the curves are highly uneven. Bass appears much quieter than midrange frequencies, while extreme highs also become less noticeable.

As listening level increases, these curves gradually flatten. Bass and treble become more proportionate to the midrange, creating a more balanced perception of the entire spectrum.

Nothing about the recording has changed. Only your hearing has.

Why Mixes Change When You Turn the Volume Down

Imagine creating a mix while monitoring at a very loud level.

The bass feels powerful. The kick is solid. Everything seems balanced.

Later, someone listens quietly on a laptop or home speakers. Suddenly the low end seems to disappear while vocals and guitars dominate.

This isn't necessarily a mixing mistake. It's a consequence of equal-loudness contours. At lower playback levels, the ear becomes less sensitive to bass, making the overall tonal balance appear different.

Why Professionals Monitor Around 75 to 85 dB SPL

Many engineers choose a reference listening level somewhere around 75 to 85 dB SPL .

This range represents a practical compromise where the ear's frequency response is relatively balanced without exposing listeners to unnecessarily high sound levels for extended periods.

Monitoring consistently at a repeatable level also helps prevent constantly adjusting EQ simply because the monitoring volume has changed.

Consistency in monitoring creates consistency in decision-making.

Loud Isn't Always Better

Listening loudly often makes almost every mix sound more exciting.

Bass appears fuller. High frequencies become more present. The entire production feels larger than life.

This can create a false sense of confidence. A mix that sounds impressive only at high volume may not translate well to everyday listening environments.

Checking your work at multiple listening levels helps reveal whether the balance remains convincing regardless of playback volume.

Equal-Loudness Contours and Earlier Concepts

Earlier articles explored how sounds interact through critical bands, frequency masking, and temporal masking.

Equal-loudness contours introduce another important layer.

Even if masking has been carefully controlled, listeners may still perceive different balances simply because they are listening louder or quieter than you were during the mix.

Good translation depends on understanding all of these psychoacoustic principles together.

Mix for Translation, Not One Playback Level

Modern music is consumed through studio monitors, headphones, televisions, smartphones, cars and portable speakers. Every environment encourages a different listening level.

Rather than optimising your mix for one specific volume, aim for a balance that remains convincing across many different playback conditions.

This approach produces mixes that feel reliable instead of impressive only under ideal circumstances.

Final Thoughts

Equal-loudness contours remind us that hearing is adaptive rather than fixed. The perceived balance of a mix changes as listening level changes, even though the recording itself remains identical.

Understanding this principle encourages more consistent monitoring, better translation and more informed EQ decisions. Instead of chasing endless tonal adjustments, you begin working with the natural behaviour of human hearing.
