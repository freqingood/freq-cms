How to Talk to a Mixing Client

Published Aug 9, 2026 · Updated Aug 11, 2026

A mixing client is trusting you with something they care about.

Sometimes they know exactly what they want.

Sometimes they have no idea how to describe what they are hearing.

Sometimes they need to ask ten questions before they feel comfortable.

And sometimes they need you to walk them through almost the entire process.

That is part of the job.

Being a good mixer is not only about making good mixes. It is also about making the person on the other side of the project feel that their music is in safe hands.

Every Client Needs Some Hand-Holding

Some clients need very little.

They send the files, references, notes, and deadline. You mix the song, send it back, and they know exactly what they are listening for.

Other clients need much more involvement.

They may want to understand every step.

They may need help preparing files.

They may want to discuss every creative decision.

They may need several conversations before they are comfortable approving a mix.

Some clients may effectively need you to spend time with them by the hour simply explaining what is happening.

None of this makes them bad clients.

They are simply at different points in their experience.

You were a beginner once too.

Always Be Patient

Patience is one of the most valuable professional skills a mixer can develop.

A client may ask a question that seems obvious to you.

Answer it.

They may use the wrong technical term.

Understand what they are trying to communicate.

They may need you to explain the same thing twice.

Explain it again.

You have spent years learning how audio works. They may have spent those years writing songs, performing, producing, or simply learning how to make music.

Everybody is a beginner once.

The fact that something is obvious to you is not a reason for someone else to feel uncomfortable asking about it.

Make the Client Feel Safe Asking Questions

A client who is afraid of looking inexperienced may stop communicating.

That is when misunderstandings begin.

If someone thinks they should already know what "headroom" means, they may nod instead of asking.

If they do not understand why you changed something, they may approve it without actually liking it.

If they are embarrassed to say they prefer the previous version, you may spend another day moving in the wrong direction.

Make it clear that questions are welcome.

The more comfortable the client is communicating honestly, the easier it becomes to make the right decisions for the song.

Assure Them of Your Skills

Clients are often nervous when they hand over a song.

They may wonder whether you will understand the music.

They may worry that the mix will change the character of the performance.

They may simply be nervous because they have never worked with a mixer before.

Part of your responsibility is to give them confidence.

Tell them what you understand about the project.

Tell them what you are going to do.

Tell them when they can expect to hear something.

Reassurance does not require a sales pitch.

You do not need to tell a client that you are the best mixer they could possibly hire.

You need to demonstrate that you understand the work and have a clear plan for it.

Never Exaggerate

Confidence and exaggeration are not the same thing.

Do not promise something you cannot guarantee.

Do not tell a client that you can make their recording sound exactly like another record.

Do not promise a result before you have heard the material.

Do not claim that a particular technique will magically solve every problem.

Professional confidence comes from knowing what you can do and being honest about what you cannot.

If you are unsure, say that you need to hear the material first.

If something requires additional work, explain it.

If the source recording has limitations, be honest about them while focusing on what can be improved.

Bite Only What You Can Chew

One of the easiest ways to damage a client relationship is to promise more than you can deliver.

If you have three projects already scheduled, do not promise another project tomorrow because you want the booking.

If a project requires more time than you expected, do not pretend it will still be finished in an unrealistic window.

If you are offering a service, make sure the scope matches what you can actually provide.

A smaller promise that you consistently keep is more valuable than a huge promise that repeatedly slips.

Your reputation is built through what happens after you say yes.

When You Are Running Late, Communicate Early

Sometimes things take longer than expected.

A mix may require more attention than you anticipated.

Files may arrive late.

A technical problem may appear.

Another project may take longer than planned.

Whatever the reason, do not disappear.

If you know the original delivery time is no longer realistic, tell the client as soon as you know.

Be honest about the situation.

Give them a realistic new expectation.

For example:

"I want to let you know that the mix is taking a little longer than I originally expected. I am not happy with the idea of rushing the final pass, so I need until tomorrow evening to finish it properly. I wanted to tell you now rather than leave you waiting without an update."

That is very different from saying nothing until the deadline has passed.

Being Honest Does Not Mean Being Defensive

If something goes wrong, explain what happened without making excuses.

There is a difference between context and justification.

The client usually does not need a long story about everything that happened during your day.

They need to know what happened, what it means for their project, and what happens next.

Keep the communication calm and direct.

Do Not Hide Problems Until They Become Bigger

If you discover that something important is missing, say so.

If the supplied files have a problem, say so.

If you need clarification before continuing, ask.

If a requested change creates a problem elsewhere, explain the trade-off.

Small problems are usually manageable.

Hidden problems become surprises.

Surprises damage trust much faster than honest conversations do.

Learn to Translate, Not Correct

Clients often describe sound in everyday language.

"The vocal needs to be bigger."

"The guitar is eating the singer."

"The chorus needs to open up."

"The drums don't feel powerful."

Do not respond by correcting their terminology.

Listen to what they are actually trying to communicate.

Then investigate the technical reason behind that perception.

The client describes the experience.

You bring the technical knowledge needed to act on it.

Respect Their Taste

You may know more about mixing than your client.

That does not mean your taste automatically outranks theirs.

They may want a vocal brighter than you would normally choose.

They may want more low end.

They may want an effect that you would not personally use.

If the choice is intentional and works for the artistic direction, it does not need to match your personal preference.

Your expertise should help the client achieve their vision, not replace it with yours.

Kindness Is a Professional Skill

Mixing can involve a lot of vulnerability.

A client is giving you something they may have spent months or years creating.

Sometimes they will be uncertain.

Sometimes they will change their mind.

Sometimes they will give you feedback that is difficult to interpret.

Sometimes they will make a mistake.

Be kind.

You do not lose authority by being patient.

You do not become less professional by being understanding.

You become someone people want to work with again.

Real Beats Impressive

The best client communication does not sound like a sales script.

It sounds like a person who knows their work and is comfortable being honest.

"I can do that."

"I need to hear the files first."

"I think there is a better way to achieve what you want."

"I need another day."

"I don't think we should change that because it is part of what makes the performance work."

"I understand what you mean. Let me try another approach."

Those statements build more trust than exaggerated promises ever will.

Your Client Should Know They Are in Safe Hands

You do not need to pretend that everything is always perfect.

You need to show that you can handle the situation when it is not.

Be patient with beginners.

Give clients the hand-holding they need.

Assure them of your skills without exaggerating.

Take on work you can actually deliver.

Communicate early when circumstances change.

Be honest about limitations.

Respect their vision.

And be kind.

The technical quality of your mix gets you noticed.

The way you handle the person behind the mix is what makes them trust you with the next one.
