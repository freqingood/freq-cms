Why Do Wideners Sound Hollow?

Published Aug 4, 2026 · Updated Aug 11, 2026

Stereo wideners can make a mix feel dramatically larger. Guitars spread outward, vocals seem to float beyond the speakers and the stereo image appears impressively expansive.

Yet many widening techniques introduce an unwanted side effect. The mix begins sounding hollow, phasey or strangely thin, especially when played in mono.

The reason is not the extra width itself. It is the psychoacoustic consequences of how that width is created.

Width Often Begins with Small Delays

In What Is the Haas Effect? , we learned that delaying one copy of a sound by a few milliseconds can make a single source appear wider.

The auditory system fuses both arrivals into one event while interpreting their timing differences as spatial information.

Used carefully, this creates convincing width without producing a distinct echo.

However, those same timing differences have another consequence.

One Sound Plus a Delayed Copy

Imagine duplicating a guitar track and delaying the copy by a few milliseconds.

Both signals now contain almost identical musical information, but they no longer reach the listener at exactly the same moment.

When these two versions combine acoustically, some frequencies reinforce one another while others partially cancel.

This creates a repeating pattern of peaks and notches known as comb filtering .

Stereo width can be created through timing differences, but timing differences also reshape the frequency spectrum.

Why Comb Filtering Sounds Hollow

Comb filtering removes energy at predictable intervals across the frequency spectrum.

Instead of hearing a smooth tonal balance, the auditory system receives a series of narrow boosts and cancellations.

Listeners often describe the result as hollow, metallic, phasey or lacking body.

Importantly, this is not because frequencies have simply become quieter. The spectral balance itself has become uneven.

Stereo Can Hide the Problem

When listening in stereo, each ear receives slightly different information.

As discussed in What Are Interaural Time and Level Differences? , the brain uses these differences to construct a stereo image.

Because the left and right channels remain partially independent, comb filtering may seem less obvious during normal stereo playback.

The increased sense of width can easily distract from subtle tonal changes.

Mono Reveals the Full Effect

Summing the same signal to mono removes the left-right separation that previously helped disguise those timing differences.

The delayed copy now combines directly with the original signal.

The comb filtering becomes much more pronounced, often causing important musical elements to lose clarity and impact.

This is why mono compatibility remains an essential translation check.

Not Every Widener Works the Same Way

Different stereo processors create width using different techniques.

Some rely primarily on short delays. Others alter phase relationships, frequency balance or decorrelation between channels.

Each approach produces different psychoacoustic results, but every method involves trade-offs between perceived width, localisation accuracy and mono compatibility.

Natural Width Usually Starts Earlier

The widest professional mixes often rely less on aggressive widening processors than many people expect.

Arrangement, complementary performances, thoughtful panning and carefully managed depth cues naturally create spaciousness before any widening effect is added.

When the mix already contains clear localisation cues, only subtle enhancement is usually required.

Ask a Different Question

Instead of asking, "How can I make this sound wider?" , consider asking, "Why doesn't this already feel spacious?"

The answer may involve frequency masking, crowded arrangements, insufficient depth or unclear stereo positioning rather than a lack of widening.

Solving those underlying problems often creates a larger, more natural stereo image than adding another processor.

Final Thoughts

Stereo wideners sound hollow when the timing differences that create apparent width also introduce comb filtering. The auditory system interprets those delays as spatial cues, but the same delays reshape the frequency spectrum whenever the signals combine.

Understanding this trade-off allows you to treat widening as a finishing touch rather than a substitute for strong arrangement, balanced localisation and convincing depth. The result is a stereo image that feels spacious while remaining stable across real-world playback systems.
