What Is Frequency Masking?

Published Aug 4, 2026 · Updated Aug 11, 2026

If your mix sounds crowded, muddy, or unclear even though every track sounds good on its own, you're probably hearing frequency masking .

Frequency masking occurs when one sound makes another harder to hear because both occupy similar regions of the frequency spectrum at the same time. The quieter sound doesn't disappear. Instead, your hearing becomes less sensitive to it, making important details feel buried in the mix.

This isn't simply a mixing problem. It's a property of human hearing, and understanding it is one of the biggest steps from beginner mixing to professional mixing.

Your Ears Don't Hear Every Frequency Independently

The cochlea inside your inner ear separates sound into approximately 24 overlapping regions called critical bands , a widely used modeling convention (Moore, Cambridge Loudness Models) rather than a fixed biological count. Sounds that fall within the same critical band compete with one another much more than sounds that are farther apart.

Imagine several people speaking in nearly identical voices. Even if none of them is particularly loud, distinguishing one conversation becomes difficult because they're competing in the same perceptual space. Instruments behave the same way when they share similar frequencies.

This is why simply adding more volume rarely improves clarity. If two instruments occupy the same critical band, making one louder often increases the masking rather than solving it.

Masking Isn't Symmetrical

One of the most important psychoacoustic principles in mixing is the upward spread of masking .

Low-frequency energy masks higher frequencies far more effectively than high frequencies mask lower ones, an asymmetry documented in research on masking-derived auditory filter shapes. This happens because the traveling wave inside the cochlea spreads toward higher-frequency regions as it moves along the basilar membrane; the exact degree of asymmetry varies with level and the specific sounds involved.

In practical terms, unnecessary energy around 200 to 400 Hz, a common low-mid region rather than a fixed psychoacoustic boundary, doesn't just make that range sound heavy. It raises the hearing threshold for frequencies above it, reducing the perceived clarity of vocals, guitars, snare attack, and cymbals. Listeners often describe the result as "mud," even though what they're really noticing is the loss of definition across the mix.

Common Examples of Frequency Masking

  • Kick drum and bass guitar competing for low-frequency energy.
  • Lead vocals sharing the same upper-midrange as guitars or keyboards.
  • Layered synthesizers occupying identical spectral ranges.
  • Dense reverbs masking vocal intelligibility through temporal overlap.
  • Multiple distorted guitars fighting for the same midrange.

None of these combinations are inherently wrong. Professional mixes contain masking too. The difference is that experienced engineers decide which instrument should dominate each region of the spectrum and allow the others to support it.

Why EQ Isn't Always the Solution

Many mixing guides suggest cutting 250 or 300 Hz whenever a mix sounds muddy. Sometimes that works, but the frequency itself isn't the problem.

The real objective is reducing unnecessary masking. Depending on the arrangement, the better solution might be changing instrument balance, adjusting the arrangement, moving a part to a different octave, automating levels, or using dynamic EQ that reacts only when masking actually occurs.

EQ is one way to create separation. It isn't the only one.

Masking Happens in Time Too

Masking isn't limited to frequencies. Our hearing also experiences temporal masking , where loud sounds briefly hide quieter sounds that occur immediately before or after them.

This is why stacked transients, such as kick and bass attacks or dense percussion, can feel less defined even when their frequency balance looks correct on an analyzer, an effect studied directly in the temporal masking research literature. Good mixing considers both spectral overlap and timing.

The Practical Rule

Before inserting another plugin, ask yourself:

Is this instrument difficult to hear because of its tone, or because another instrument is occupying the same perceptual space?

That single question often leads to better mix decisions than blindly adding EQ or compression.

Professional mixing isn't about making every instrument louder. It's about giving every important element enough perceptual space for the listener to hear it naturally.
